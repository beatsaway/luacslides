==========================================================================
YEAR 7 · MAKE IT RESPOND
==========================================================================

On/off, one input, a rule, a number kept, a machine with states.

By the end of Year 7 a pupil can switch an output from code, read a button
safely, combine two conditions, keep a count that survives, and design a
short sequence of states.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Y7.1  Make a light blink
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

  Code turns a pin on and off. A resistor protects the LED.

  Inquiry: How do you use code to turn a light on and off?

  Aim: By the end you will make an LED blink, and explain why it needs a
  resistor.

  [idea] A pin is on or off
    HIGH means 5 V, so the LED is on. LOW means 0 V, so the LED is off.
    The resistor stays in the circuit either way. It limits the current.

  [build] A blinking light
    1. Drag an Arduino Uno and a breadboard onto the canvas.
    2. Add an LED. The long leg is the positive one.
    3. Run a 220 Ω resistor from pin 13 to the long leg.
    4. Wire the short leg to a GND pin.

  [code] Six lines, one blinking light. Decoded: pinMode(13, OUTPUT);
  digitalWrite(13, HIGH); delay(500).

  [predict] An LED is connected to a 5 V pin with no resistor. What
  happens?
    Answer: It lights — and then it burns out.

  [debug] The LED is in the breadboard, the code is uploaded, and nothing
  lights.
    Fault: The LED is in backwards.

  [check] What does digitalWrite(13, HIGH) do to pin 13?
    A. Reads the voltage on pin 13
    B. Connects pin 13 to 5 V
    C. Connects pin 13 to GND
    Answer: B. It connects pin 13 to 5 V.
    HIGH is 5 V, LOW is 0 V, and a digital pin can do nothing else.
    Reading is a different instruction: digitalRead.

  [exit] HIGH means the pin is at 5 V. The resistor limits the ________.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Y7.2  A button that lights a lamp
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

  A light that comes on only while you hold the button.

  Inquiry: How does the board know that a button has been pressed?

  Aim: By the end you will make a light come on while a button is held,
  and explain why the pin needs a resistor to 0 V.

  [idea] Give the pin a known voltage
    A resistor to 0 V holds the pin LOW while the button is open. Press
    the button and the pin is connected to 5 V, so digitalRead returns
    HIGH.

  [build] A press-light
    1. Push the button so it straddles the centre trench.
    2. One side to 5 V, the other side to pin 2.
    3. 10 kΩ from pin 2 down to GND.
    4. LED and 220 Ω from pin 13 to GND.

  [code] Read it, then use it. Decoded: pinMode(2, INPUT); digitalRead(2);
  digitalWrite(13, pressed).

  [predict] A button is wired to pin 2, and nothing else is on pin 2. The
  button is open. What does digitalRead(2) return?
    Answer: It changes at random.

  [debug] The LED is on all the time, and the Monitor shows 1, even with
  nothing pressed.
    Fault: The button is shorted across its own legs.

  [check] Why does pin 2 need the 10 kΩ resistor?
    A. To protect the button from too much current
    B. To hold the pin at 0 V when the button is open
    C. To make the LED brighter
    Answer: B. To hold the pin at 0 V when the button is open.
    The button is the only path to 5 V, and it is open. The resistor is
    the only path to 0 V, and it is always there. Without it the reading
    jumps about.

  [exit] When the button is not pressed, the resistor holds the pin at
  ________.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Y7.3  Two things must be true
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

  Two inputs. One rule, written in words before the code.

  Inquiry: The door is open and you press the button. Why should the lift
  stay still?

  Aim: By the end you will use AND and OR, and write the rule in words
  before you write the code.

  [idea] And means both. Or means either.
    && is true only when both inputs are true. || is true when at least
    one input is true. Write that rule as a sentence, then type it.

  [build] Lift safety
    1. Button A to pin 2, button B to pin 3.
    2. A 10 kΩ pull-down from each pin to GND.
    3. LED and 220 Ω on pin 13.
    4. Fill in the four-row truth table in your book.

  [code] One rule, two inputs. Decoded: &&; ||; else.

  [predict] The motor must run only when the door is shut AND the button
  is pressed. Which line does that?
    Answer: The second one: &&.

  [debug] The LED comes on when either button is pressed, not only when
  both are.
    Fault: The || on the if line.

  [check] Which operator is true when at least one of two buttons is
  pressed?
    A. &&
    B. ||
    C. !
    Answer: B. || — or.
    || is false only when both sides are false. ! is not a joiner: it
    flips one condition, so !HIGH is LOW.

  [exit] The lift moves only when the door is shut ________ the button is
  pressed.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Y7.4  Keep a count
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

  A number that keeps going up, instead of starting again.

  Inquiry: How can a program remember how many times the button was
  pressed?

  Aim: By the end you will keep a count that goes up, and find the mistake
  that sets it back to 0.

  [idea] Where you create the variable matters
    Create the count outside loop() and it keeps its value. Create it
    inside loop() and it goes back to 0 every time.

  [build] A press counter
    1. Keep the button and pull-down from Y7.2 on pin 2.
    2. Add an LED and 220 Ω on pin 13.
    3. Open the Serial Monitor and set it to 9600 baud.
    4. Write int count = 0; above setup().

  [code] One line that keeps a number. Decoded: int count = 0;; count =
  count + 1;; delay(250);.

  [predict] This loop runs with int count = 0; on its first line. You
  press the button three times. What prints?
    Answer: 1, then 0, then 1, then 0…

  [debug] Every press prints 1. The number never goes above it.
    Fault: int count = 0; is inside loop().

  [check] Where must a counter be declared so it survives from one loop()
  to the next?
    A. Inside loop()
    B. Outside both functions, at the top
    C. Inside setup()
    Answer: B. Outside both functions, at the top of the file.
    A variable made inside a function lives only while that function runs.
    At the top of the file it lives for as long as the program.

  [exit] A count keeps going up when the variable is created ________
  loop().

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Y7.5  Design a crossing
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

  A list of stages. Each stage is which lamps are on, and for how long.

  Inquiry: How does a traffic light change on its own, with nobody
  pressing a switch?

  Aim: By the end you will list the stages of a traffic light, build it,
  and set how long each stage lasts.

  [idea] A stage is a pattern and a time
    Write each stage as which lamps are on and how many milliseconds it
    lasts. The loop runs the list from the top, then repeats.

  [build] A traffic light
    1. Red on pin 11, amber on 10, green on 9.
    2. Every LED gets its own 220 Ω resistor.
    3. All three short legs share the GND rail.
    4. Draw the states and the times in your book first.

  [code] Four states, in order. Decoded: Every state sets every pin;
  delay(6000); Order matters.

  [predict] A traffic light has three LEDs. How many can be on at the same
  time?
    Answer: Sometimes two — red and amber together.

  [debug] Green and red are lit at the same time.
    Fault: One digitalWrite(..., LOW) is missing.

  [check] What is a state?
    A. A delay
    B. The set of outputs at one moment
    C. A pin number
    Answer: B. The set of outputs at one moment.
    A state is the whole picture: which lamps are on right now. A machine
    is a list of states and the rules for moving between them.

  [exit] A traffic light program is a list of ________ and the time for
  each one.


==========================================================================
YEAR 8 · MEASURE IT
==========================================================================

Ranges, sensors, calibration, and a device that decides for itself.

By the end of Year 8 a pupil can read a range, explain why a sensor needs
a divider, use PWM, turn a raw number into a real unit, and build a device
with two limits so a small wobble does not switch it on and off.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Y8.1  Read a knob
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

  A number from 0 to 1023, not just on or off.

  Inquiry: A switch is only on or off. How can a knob tell the board how
  far you turned it?

  Aim: By the end you will read a knob as a number from 0 to 1023, and say
  what that number means.

  [idea] A number, not just on or off
    analogRead gives a whole number from 0 to 1023. That number stands for
    a voltage between 0 V and 5 V.

  [build] A dimmer dial
    1. Outer legs of the pot to 5 V and GND.
    2. Middle pin of the knob to A0.
    3. LED and 220 Ω on pin 9.
    4. Turn the knob and watch the Serial Monitor.

  [code] Read the dial, print the number. Decoded: analogRead(A0);
  map(raw, 0, 1023, 0, 100); Serial.println(percent).

  [predict] The dial is exactly halfway. What does analogRead(A0) return?
    Answer: About 512.

  [debug] The reading jumps between 0 and 1023, with nothing in between.
    Fault: The middle pin of the knob is not connected to A0.

  [check] analogRead(A0) returns 512. What is the voltage on A0?
    A. 512 V
    B. About 2.5 V
    C. 0.5 V
    Answer: B. About 2.5 V.
    512 is about half of 1023, so the voltage is about half of 5 V. The
    reading is a step number, and the voltage is what it is counting.

  [exit] An analog pin gives a ________ from 0 to 1023, not just on or
  off.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Y8.2  A light that comes on in the dark
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

  The sensor changes resistance. A second resistor makes a voltage the
  board can read.

  Inquiry: How can a light come on by itself when the room gets dark?

  Aim: By the end you will use a light sensor to get a number, and explain
  why it needs a second resistor.

  [idea] The board reads voltage
    An LDR changes resistance as the light changes. On its own that does
    not give the pin a voltage. A second resistor makes a divider, so the
    reading changes with the light.

  [build] A night light
    1. 5 V to one leg of the LDR.
    2. The other leg of the LDR to A0.
    3. 10 kΩ from A0 down to GND.
    4. LED and 220 Ω on pin 9.

  [code] Sensor, rule, action. Decoded: int light = analogRead(A0);; if
  (light < 300); digitalWrite(9, HIGH).

  [predict] An LDR is the top half of a voltage divider. You shine a light
  on it. What happens to the voltage at the middle?
    Answer: It rises.

  [debug] The reading is stuck at 1023 and the LED never comes on,
  whatever you do.
    Fault: There is no path from A0 down to GND.

  [check] Why can't the LDR be connected straight from 5 V to A0?
    A. It would use too much current
    B. A0 measures voltage, and the LDR only changes resistance
    C. The LDR would burn out
    Answer: B. Because A0 measures voltage, and the LDR only changes
    resistance.
    On its own, the LDR would put almost 5 V on A0 whatever the light. The
    second resistor is what turns a change in resistance into a change in
    voltage.

  [exit] The board reads ________. The second resistor turns a change in
  resistance into that.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Y8.3  Dim an LED
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

  The pin is still only on or off. It is on for part of the time.

  Inquiry: A pin is only fully on or fully off. How can an LED look dim?

  Aim: By the end you will dim an LED, and explain that the pin is never
  at a voltage in between.

  [idea] On for part of the time
    analogWrite switches the pin on and off thousands of times a second. A
    low number means on for a small part of each cycle, so the LED looks
    dim. The pin is never at a voltage in between.

  [build] A dimmer
    1. Pot on A0, outer legs to 5 V and GND.
    2. LED and 220 Ω on pin 6 — a PWM pin.
    3. Pins 3, 5, 6, 9, 10, 11 can dim.
    4. Print the value you send to the pin.

  [code] From a dial to a duty cycle. Decoded: analogWrite(6, level);
  map(knob, 0, 1023, 0, 255); pin 6.

  [predict] analogWrite(6, 128) sends a 50% signal to an LED. What is the
  voltage on pin 6?
    Answer: 5 V half the time, 0 V half the time.

  [debug] The LED is either fully on or fully off. The knob does nothing
  in between.
    Fault: Pin 7 cannot do PWM.

  [check] A pin is HIGH for 1 ms and LOW for 3 ms, over and over. What is
  the average voltage?
    A. 1.25 V
    B. 2.5 V
    C. 3.75 V
    Answer: A. 1.25 V.
    It is on for one quarter of the time. One quarter of 5 V is 1.25 V.
    That fraction is called the duty cycle.

  [exit] The LED looks dim because the pin is on for only part of the
  ________.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Y8.4  A number that means degrees
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

  0.5 V means 0 °C. Then 0.01 V for each degree.

  Inquiry: The sensor gives a voltage. How do we turn that into a
  temperature in °C?

  Aim: By the end you will convert the reading to degrees Celsius, and
  explain why the formula subtracts 0.5.

  [idea] From volts to degrees
    Read the voltage. Subtract 0.5 V, because that is what the sensor
    outputs at 0 °C. Multiply by 100, because each extra 0.01 V is one
    degree.

  [build] A thermometer
    1. Hold the TMP36 with the flat face towards you.
    2. Left leg to 5 V, right leg to GND.
    3. Middle leg to A0.
    4. Open the Serial Monitor at 9600.

  [code] Three lines from a reading to a temperature. Decoded: raw * 5.0 /
  1024.0; (volts - 0.5); * 100.0.

  [predict] A TMP36 outputs 0.75 V at 25 °C. What will analogRead show?
    Answer: About 153.

  [debug] The temperature reads −50.00 C, every single time.
    Fault: raw * 5 / 1024 is done in whole numbers, so it is always 0.

  [check] Why does the formula subtract 0.5?
    A. To correct for room temperature
    B. The sensor outputs 0.5 V at 0 °C
    C. To convert volts to millivolts
    Answer: B. Because the sensor outputs 0.5 V at 0 °C.
    The output is offset by half a volt so the sensor can report
    temperatures below zero. Without subtracting it, 0 °C would read as 50
    °C.

  [exit] We subtract 0.5 because the sensor outputs 0.5 V at ________.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Y8.5  An alarm that stays steady
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

  Two limits, with a gap, so a small wobble does not switch the buzzer.

  Inquiry: Why does the buzzer keep switching on and off near the limit,
  and how do you stop it?

  Aim: By the end you will turn a buzzer on when the soil is dry, and keep
  it off until the soil is wet enough.

  [idea] Two limits, with a gap
    Turn the buzzer on when the reading goes below the lower limit. Leave
    it on until the reading goes above the higher limit. Between the two,
    do not change anything.

  [build] A plant alarm
    1. Probe into A0 and 5 V (two nails work).
    2. 10 kΩ from A0 down to GND.
    3. Buzzer on pin 8, LED on pin 9.
    4. Test in dry air, then in a wet cup.

  [code] A flag, two rules, two outputs. Decoded: if (wetness < 380) dry =
  1;; if (wetness > 420) dry = 0;; digitalWrite(8, dry).

  [predict] The threshold is 400. The reading wobbles between 398 and 402.
  What does the buzzer do?
    Answer: It keeps switching on and off.

  [debug] The buzzer is on when the soil is wet, and off when it is dry.
    Fault: The probe is the other way round in the divider: wet now reads
    low and dry reads high.

  [check] Why two thresholds instead of one?
    A. To make the code longer
    B. So a reading that wobbles cannot make the buzzer switch on and off
    C. Because analogRead is noisy
    Answer: B. So a reading that wobbles cannot make the buzzer switch on
    and off.
    One limit makes the buzzer switch every time the reading wobbles
    across it. A gap between two limits means it changes only when the
    reading has really moved.

  [exit] The buzzer stays steady because there are two ________, not one.


==========================================================================
YEAR 9 · MAKE IT MOVE
==========================================================================

Current, power switching, position, feedback, and a message.

By the end of Year 9 a pupil can explain why a pin cannot drive a motor,
switch a big current with a transistor, command a position, close a
feedback loop, and send a reading as a formatted message.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Y9.1  A motor needs more than a pin
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

  A pin can light an LED. It cannot power a motor on its own.

  Inquiry: Why can't a pin power a motor the way it powers an LED?

  Aim: By the end you will switch a motor on with a transistor, and
  explain why the pin cannot supply the motor's current.

  [idea] The pin sends the signal
    A pin can give about 20 mA. A motor needs more than that. The pin
    switches a transistor on and off. The transistor connects the motor to
    the supply. A diode gives the motor's current a safe path when it
    switches off.

  [build] A motor driver
    1. Motor and a separate 5 V supply.
    2. NPN: collector to the motor, emitter to GND.
    3. 1 kΩ from pin 9 to the base.
    4. Diode across the motor, and join both grounds.

  [code] The pin only says on or off. Decoded: digitalWrite(9, HIGH); the
  transistor; delay(2000).

  [predict] A motor needs 400 mA. A pin can safely give 20 mA. What
  happens if you connect the motor straight to the pin?
    Answer: The pin overheats and may be destroyed.

  [debug] The motor does not turn. The transistor is wired correctly and
  the code runs.
    Fault: There is no common ground.

  [check] Why is there a diode across the motor?
    A. To stop the motor spinning backwards
    B. To give the motor's collapsing magnetic field a safe path
    C. To reduce the current
    Answer: B. To give the collapsing magnetic field a safe path.
    A motor is a coil. Switch it off and the field collapses, and the coil
    generates a voltage spike that can destroy the transistor. The diode
    sends that current round the motor instead.

  [exit] The pin sends the signal. The ________ carries the motor's
  current.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Y9.2  Control the speed
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

  More of the time on means more power to the motor.

  Inquiry: How do you change a motor's speed, not just turn it on or off?

  Aim: By the end you will set a motor's speed from a knob, and explain
  why half the time on is not exactly half the speed.

  [idea] More time on, more power
    analogWrite still switches fully on and fully off. A higher value
    means the motor is powered for more of each cycle, so it spins faster.
    It will not be exactly half speed at 128, because the motor has to
    overcome friction.

  [build] A speed control
    1. Keep the transistor and motor from Y9.1.
    2. Knob: outer pins to 5 V and GND, middle pin to A0.
    3. Drive the base from pin 9 — a PWM pin.
    4. Keep the motor supply separate, grounds joined.

  [code] A dial on the base. Decoded: analogWrite(9, speed); map(..., 0,
  255); delay(50).

  [predict] You send 50% PWM to a motor. Is it exactly half speed?
    Answer: Roughly half, but not exactly.

  [debug] The motor runs at full speed whatever the knob does.
    Fault: The motor's negative lead goes straight to ground, so the
    transistor is bypassed.

  [check] At 25% duty, what fraction of the supply voltage does the motor
  see on average?
    A. A quarter
    B. A half
    C. All of it
    Answer: A. A quarter.
    The pin is on for a quarter of each cycle, so the average is a quarter
    of the supply. That is why 25% duty feels much slower than half.

  [exit] PWM changes how much of the ________ the motor is powered for.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Y9.3  Set a servo to an angle
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

  You send an angle. The servo moves there and stays.

  Inquiry: How do you tell a motor to go to an angle and stay there?

  Aim: By the end you will set a servo to an angle you choose, and explain
  that the signal is the angle, not the speed.

  [idea] The signal is the angle
    A servo expects a pulse about every 20 ms. A 1 ms pulse means about
    0°. 1.5 ms means about 90°. 2 ms means about 180°. You tell it where
    to go, not how fast to spin.

  [build] A dial-controlled arm
    1. Signal wire to pin 9.
    2. Power from a separate 5 V supply.
    3. Join the grounds.
    4. Pot on A0 to choose the angle.

  [code] Ask for an angle. Decoded: #include <Servo.h>; arm.attach(9);;
  arm.write(angle);.

  [predict] A servo gets a pulse every 20 ms. What does the width of the
  pulse tell it?
    Answer: What angle to hold.

  [debug] The servo twitches, and sometimes the whole board resets when it
  moves.
    Fault: The servo is taking its current from the board.

  [check] A pulse of 1.5 ms means what?
    A. 0°
    B. 90°
    C. 180°
    Answer: B. 90°.
    1.5 ms is halfway between 1.0 and 2.0 ms, and halfway between 0° and
    180° is 90°.

  [exit] A servo is told an ________, not a speed.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Y9.4  Turn towards the light
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

  Read the sensors, move a little, then check again.

  Inquiry: How can the lamp turn itself towards the light, then stop?

  Aim: By the end you will make a servo move towards the brighter sensor
  and stop when the two readings are close.

  [idea] Move, then check again
    The error is the difference between the two light readings. If the
    error is large, move the servo a step towards the brighter sensor. If
    the error is small, stop. Then read the sensors again.

  [build] A light tracker
    1. Two LDRs, left and right, into A0 and A1.
    2. Each needs its own 10 kΩ to GND.
    3. Servo signal on pin 9, separate supply.
    4. Shade one sensor and watch the arm turn.

  [code] The loop, in twelve lines. Decoded: left > right + 40; angle =
  angle - 2; constrain(angle, 0, 180).

  [predict] The two light readings are 600 and 610. What should the servo
  do?
    Answer: Stop, because the readings are close.

  [debug] The arm swings past the light and keeps going back and forth.
    Fault: The gap is too small for how much the two sensors disagree.

  [check] In this program, what is the error?
    A. A mistake in the code
    B. The difference between the two light readings
    C. The delay between readings
    Answer: B. The difference between the two light readings.
    The error is how far apart the two readings are. The program tries to
    make that smaller. When the difference is under 40, it stops.

  [exit] The error is the difference between the two ________.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Y9.5  Send a reading
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

  Each reading is a name, a value, and a new line.

  Inquiry: How do you send a reading so another program can tell the
  readings apart?

  Aim: By the end you will print each reading on its own line, with a name
  and a value.

  [idea] Name, value, new line
    Each message has a name, a value, and a new line. That agreed shape is
    a protocol. The other program reads one line, splits the name from the
    value, and knows which reading it is.

  [build] A reporting sensor
    1. Use any sensor from Year 8 on A0.
    2. Serial at 9600 baud.
    3. One line per reading, with a label per value.
    4. Read it in the Monitor, or on a second board.

  [code] Labels, values, and one newline. Decoded: print vs println; L:
  and V:; volts, 2.

  [predict] The board prints 23.5, then 23.4, then 23.6, with nothing
  between them. What does the receiver see?
    Answer: One long number.

  [debug] The Monitor shows L:512,V:2.50L:498,V:2.43L:… all on one line.
    Fault: There is no newline at the end of the message.

  [check] Why does a message need an end marker?
    A. To make it longer
    B. So the receiver knows where one reading stops and the next begins
    C. To slow the data down
    Answer: B. So the receiver knows where one reading stops and the next
    begins.
    A stream of characters has no natural boundaries. A newline, a length
    or a fixed size is what turns characters into messages.

  [exit] Each reading is separate because the message ends with a
  ________.

WHAT A PUPIL CAN DO AT THE END OF EACH YEAR
==========================================================================

Year 7, Make it respond: By the end of Year 7 a pupil can switch an output
from code, read a button safely, combine two conditions, keep a count that
survives, and design a short sequence of states.

Year 8, Measure it: By the end of Year 8 a pupil can read a range, explain
why a sensor needs a divider, use PWM, turn a raw number into a real unit,
and build a device with two limits so a small wobble does not switch it on
and off.

Year 9, Make it move: By the end of Year 9 a pupil can explain why a pin
cannot drive a motor, switch a big current with a transistor, command a
position, close a feedback loop, and send a reading as a formatted
message.

Year 7 to 8 and 8 to 9 are not prerequisites in the administrative
sense: a class that starts at Year 9 still gets a complete unit, with
the Year 8 ideas it needs restated on the starter slide. The three
years are written as one arc for a pupil who does all three.
